


# Every Engineer Wants Kubernetes—Until They Have to Operate It

## Part 3: Why Kubernetes Failures Rarely Stay Inside Kubernetes

*"Kubernetes incidents are difficult because the platform is only one layer of the system. The failure may begin in Kubernetes, but the impact usually spreads across applications, networks, storage, and teams."*

In Part 1, we explored why Kubernetes looks like the answer to everything.

In Part 2, we examined the operational burden behind the control plane.

Now we reach the part that makes Kubernetes incidents especially difficult:

The failure rarely stays inside Kubernetes.

A Pod may be healthy, but the Service has no endpoints.

A Service may be correct, but DNS is failing.

DNS may be healthy, but a NetworkPolicy is blocking traffic.

The network may work, but the application is not ready.

The application may be ready, but storage latency is causing timeouts.

The cluster may be healthy, but the cloud load balancer is routing traffic incorrectly.

This is why Kubernetes incidents are often misdiagnosed.

They do not arrive labeled as Kubernetes failures.

They arrive as:

* Slow checkout
* Failed login
* Missing data
* Connection timeout
* Broken deployment
* Unavailable API
* Random restarts
* Partial customer impact

The customer sees one broken service.

Engineers see many possible layers.

That gap between visible impact and hidden cause is what makes Kubernetes troubleshooting so demanding.

---

# The Full Traffic Path Matters

When a customer sends a request to an application running on Kubernetes, the request may travel through several layers:

1. DNS
2. External load balancer
3. Ingress controller or gateway
4. Kubernetes Service
5. Endpoint or EndpointSlice
6. Pod network
7. Container port
8. Application process
9. Downstream dependencies
10. Database, queue, or storage

Every one of these layers can fail.

The application team may see healthy Pods.

The platform team may see healthy nodes.

The customer may still receive an error.

This creates one of the most important Kubernetes troubleshooting principles:

> **Do not troubleshoot only the layer where the symptom appears. Trace the complete path.**

A failed request is not proof that the application is broken.

A healthy Pod is not proof that the service is working.

A running container is only one piece of the system.

---

# Kubernetes Failures Often Look Like Application Failures

Imagine a user cannot complete a purchase.

The application logs show connection timeouts.

The natural assumption is that the payment service is unhealthy.

But the actual cause may be:

* The Service selector no longer matches the Pods
* The Ingress backend points to the wrong port
* CoreDNS is failing
* A NetworkPolicy blocks traffic
* A readiness probe removed all endpoints
* The node network is degraded
* The Pod is running but listening on the wrong port

The application reports the symptom.

Kubernetes may contain the cause.

This is why troubleshooting requires evidence across layers.

Without that evidence, teams often restart workloads and hope the problem disappears.

Sometimes it does.

That does not mean the root cause was understood.

---

# Service and Selector Mistakes

A Kubernetes Service routes traffic to Pods selected by labels.

This mechanism is simple.

It is also a common source of failure.

A Service may specify:

```yaml
selector:
  app: payment
```

But the Pods may use:

```yaml
labels:
  app: payments
```

One extra letter is enough to break the entire path.

The Pods are healthy.

The Service exists.

The application is unreachable.

The Service has no endpoints.

This is a classic example of Kubernetes failing silently from the customer's perspective.

Nothing necessarily crashes.

Traffic simply has nowhere to go.

Teams should always verify:

* Service selectors
* Pod labels
* Endpoints
* EndpointSlices
* Target ports
* Container ports

A Service without endpoints is not a networking mystery.

It is usually a selection or readiness problem.

---

# Wrong Ports Create Invisible Failures

Port configuration appears in several places:

* Container port
* Application listening port
* Service port
* Target port
* Ingress backend port
* Load-balancer health-check port

These values do not always need to match, but they must align correctly.

A common failure occurs when:

* The application listens on port 8080
* The Service targets port 8000
* The Pod is healthy
* Traffic always fails

From Kubernetes' perspective, the workload may be running normally.

From the customer's perspective, the service is down.

Port mismatches are simple errors with production-level consequences.

---

# Readiness Probe Failures Remove Traffic

Readiness probes decide whether a Pod should receive traffic.

This is one of Kubernetes' most important safety mechanisms.

A Pod can be running but not ready.

If the readiness probe fails, Kubernetes removes the Pod from Service endpoints.

This prevents customers from reaching an unhealthy workload.

But poorly designed readiness probes can also create outages.

Suppose a readiness probe checks a non-critical dependency.

That dependency becomes slow.

Every Pod fails readiness.

Kubernetes removes every Pod from the Service.

The application becomes unavailable even though it could have continued serving partial functionality.

This creates an important design question:

> **What does ready actually mean?**

A readiness probe should represent whether the application can safely serve traffic.

It should not fail because of every minor dependency problem.

Overly strict probes can amplify small failures into full outages.

---

# Liveness Probes Can Create Restart Loops

Liveness probes determine whether a container should be restarted.

They are useful when an application becomes stuck and cannot recover on its own.

But a bad liveness probe can become destructive.

Imagine an application starts slowly under high load.

The liveness probe begins too early.

The probe fails.

Kubernetes restarts the container.

The application starts again.

The probe fails again.

The container enters a restart loop.

The platform is trying to heal the workload.

The healing action prevents recovery.

This is why liveness probes should be conservative.

They should detect unrecoverable states.

They should not be used as a generic performance check.

---

# Startup Probes Protect Slow Applications

Startup probes exist for applications that require more time to initialize.

Without them, liveness and readiness checks may begin before the application has fully started.

This is especially important for:

* Large Java applications
* Applications performing migrations
* Services loading models
* Legacy systems
* Workloads with expensive startup tasks

A startup probe gives the application time to initialize before other probes take control.

Without it, Kubernetes may interpret slow startup as failure.

The platform is not wrong.

The health model is incomplete.

---

# Scheduling Failures Look Like Deployment Failures

A deployment may appear broken because Pods remain Pending.

The application image is fine.

The manifest is valid.

The scheduler cannot place the workload.

Possible causes include:

* Insufficient CPU
* Insufficient memory
* Node selectors
* Affinity rules
* Taints
* Missing tolerations
* Volume-zone restrictions
* Pod anti-affinity
* Resource quotas

A Pending Pod is not necessarily a container problem.

It is a placement problem.

Engineers should inspect scheduler events before changing application code.

Kubernetes often explains the failure directly.

The challenge is knowing where to look.

---

# Resource Requests Can Block Scheduling

Suppose a Pod requests:

* 8 CPUs
* 16 GiB memory

The cluster has enough total capacity, but no single node has that much free capacity.

The Pod remains Pending.

Adding more replicas may make the problem worse.

The scheduler requires a node that can satisfy each Pod's request.

Cluster-wide capacity does not guarantee schedulable capacity.

This is another reason resource requests must reflect actual needs.

Requests that are too high waste capacity and block deployments.

Requests that are too low increase runtime instability.

---

# Affinity Rules Can Overconstrain the Cluster

Affinity and anti-affinity rules control where Pods are scheduled.

They are useful for resilience and performance.

They can also become too restrictive.

For example, a team may require:

* One Pod per node
* Only specific availability zones
* Only nodes with certain labels
* Separation from several other workloads

Individually, each rule appears reasonable.

Together, they may make scheduling impossible.

The deployment fails because the platform has no valid placement.

Strong scheduling policy requires balancing resilience with practical capacity.

---

# Resource Pressure Changes Workload Behavior

A node under pressure may begin evicting workloads.

Common pressure conditions include:

* Memory pressure
* Disk pressure
* PID pressure

Kubernetes protects the node by removing Pods.

The application team sees restarts.

The platform team sees resource exhaustion.

The customer sees instability.

Node pressure is especially dangerous when several workloads are affected at once.

One noisy workload can cause unrelated services to restart.

This is why resource isolation matters.

Requests, limits, priority classes, and workload placement all influence which applications survive under pressure.

---

# OOMKilled Is a Symptom, Not a Root Cause

When a container exceeds its memory limit, it may be terminated with an `OOMKilled` status.

This is often treated as a simple configuration problem.

Sometimes it is.

But the real cause may include:

* Memory leak
* Traffic spike
* Large payloads
* Cache growth
* Poor request limits
* Incorrect memory limit
* Unexpected concurrency
* Dependency behavior

Increasing the limit may restore service temporarily.

It may not solve the underlying issue.

A mature investigation asks:

* Did application memory grow gradually?
* Did request volume change?
* Did the workload release memory?
* Did a deployment introduce new behavior?
* Did the node experience pressure?
* Was the limit based on measured usage?

The restart is visible.

The reason requires investigation.

---

# CPU Throttling Creates Slow Failures

CPU limits do not usually kill containers.

They throttle them.

This creates a different failure pattern.

The Pod remains running.

Health checks may pass.

Latency increases.

Timeouts appear.

Queues grow.

The system degrades gradually.

Because nothing crashes, CPU throttling can be harder to identify than an OOM kill.

The application may look healthy while customers experience poor performance.

This is one reason teams should monitor both CPU usage and throttling.

Running is not the same as performing.

---

# Evictions Can Trigger Cascading Failures

When Pods are evicted, Kubernetes reschedules them.

That sounds safe.

But recovery may increase pressure elsewhere.

New Pods:

* Pull images
* Mount volumes
* Connect to databases
* Rebuild caches
* Re-register with dependencies
* Generate startup traffic

If many Pods restart at once, they can overwhelm downstream systems.

A node failure may therefore become:

1. Pod eviction
2. Mass restart
3. Database connection spike
4. Dependency overload
5. Wider application outage

This is a cascading failure.

The initial infrastructure event may be small.

The recovery behavior makes it large.

---

# NetworkPolicies Create Invisible Walls

NetworkPolicies control which Pods may communicate.

They are essential for security.

They also create a common troubleshooting challenge.

A policy can block traffic without affecting Pod health.

The source Pod runs.

The destination Pod runs.

DNS resolves.

The connection still fails.

Network-policy issues often occur after:

* New namespace creation
* Label changes
* Default-deny adoption
* Policy refactoring
* Service migration

Troubleshooting requires understanding both source and destination policies.

It also requires knowing whether the CNI actually enforces them.

A policy that exists but is not enforced provides false confidence.

A policy that is enforced incorrectly creates invisible outages.

---

# DNS Failures Spread Quickly

Kubernetes services depend heavily on DNS.

A single CoreDNS issue can affect many applications at once.

Symptoms may include:

* Connection timeouts
* Slow startup
* Failed dependency calls
* Random request failures
* Increased retries
* Long response times

Because many teams rely on the same DNS layer, one failure can appear as several unrelated application incidents.

This makes DNS a shared-risk component.

Teams should monitor:

* Query latency
* Error rate
* Cache behavior
* CoreDNS resource usage
* Upstream resolver health
* Pod distribution
* Restart count

DNS is small in architecture diagrams.

Its blast radius is not.

---

# Storage Failures Create Partial Outages

Stateful applications depend on storage.

Storage failures may affect only part of the platform.

One Pod cannot mount a volume.

Another Pod runs normally.

One availability zone is affected.

Another remains healthy.

The result may be partial customer impact.

Possible causes include:

* Volume attachment failure
* CSI driver issue
* Zone mismatch
* File-system corruption
* Performance bottleneck
* Capacity exhaustion
* Snapshot failure

These failures are difficult because the workload may appear healthy until it needs storage.

A Pod can be scheduled successfully but remain stuck during mount.

The incident crosses Kubernetes, cloud infrastructure, and application data.

---

# Availability Zones Create Placement Constraints

Many cloud volumes are tied to one availability zone.

If a Pod moves to a node in another zone, the volume may not attach.

The scheduler must respect the volume's location.

This can create failures during:

* Node replacement
* Zone outage
* Cluster scaling
* StatefulSet rescheduling

Teams may assume Kubernetes can move any workload anywhere.

Storage often limits that freedom.

The platform orchestrates around infrastructure constraints.

It does not remove them.

---

# Ingress Failures Affect Healthy Services

A Service can be healthy while external users cannot reach it.

The problem may exist in the Ingress layer.

Common causes include:

* Wrong hostname
* Incorrect path
* Missing TLS certificate
* Invalid annotation
* Controller failure
* Incorrect backend port
* Cloud load-balancer issue
* Health-check mismatch

Internal requests may succeed.

External traffic may fail.

This creates misleading evidence.

Application teams test from inside the cluster and see success.

Customers outside the cluster remain affected.

Troubleshooting must include both internal and external paths.

---

# Load-Balancer Health Checks Can Remove Healthy Targets

Cloud load balancers often perform their own health checks.

Those checks may differ from Kubernetes probes.

A Pod may be ready inside the cluster.

The load balancer may still mark the backend unhealthy because:

* The health-check path is wrong
* The health-check port is wrong
* The response code is unexpected
* Security rules block the check
* The backend responds too slowly

Now two health systems disagree.

Kubernetes believes the workload is ready.

The external load balancer refuses to send traffic.

This is why end-to-end validation matters.

---

# Cascading Failures Are Usually Multi-Layered

A Kubernetes outage may begin with one problem and spread through several layers.

For example:

1. Database latency increases.
2. Application requests slow down.
3. Readiness probes fail.
4. Pods are removed from Service endpoints.
5. Remaining Pods receive more traffic.
6. CPU throttling increases.
7. More probes fail.
8. The entire service becomes unavailable.

No single component caused the full outage.

The interaction created it.

This is why production incidents require systems thinking.

The team must ask not only:

> What failed first?

But also:

> What caused the failure to spread?

---

# Retries Can Turn Degradation Into Outage

Retries are useful when failures are brief.

They are dangerous when the dependency is already overloaded.

Suppose a service times out.

Every client retries three times.

Traffic effectively multiplies.

The dependency becomes even slower.

More requests time out.

More retries are created.

The system collapses under retry amplification.

Kubernetes may then scale the calling service, adding even more traffic.

Each component behaves as configured.

The overall system becomes less stable.

Resilience patterns must be designed together.

Timeouts, retries, backoff, circuit breakers, and rate limits should work as one system.

---

# Rolling Deployments Can Break Healthy Systems

Rolling deployments gradually replace old Pods with new ones.

This reduces deployment risk.

It does not eliminate it.

A rollout may fail because:

* New Pods never become ready
* Old Pods terminate too quickly
* The database migration is incompatible
* The new version breaks API compatibility
* Resource requests prevent scheduling
* Readiness checks are incorrect
* Traffic shifts before caches warm

Kubernetes manages the rollout mechanics.

It cannot determine whether the new application behavior is safe.

A technically successful rollout can still create customer impact.

---

# ConfigMaps and Secrets Can Trigger Widespread Failure

A configuration change may affect many workloads.

One incorrect ConfigMap value can cause:

* Failed startup
* Wrong endpoints
* Authentication errors
* Feature failures
* Invalid timeouts
* Broken integrations

Secret changes can also create outages.

A rotated credential may not be updated everywhere.

A certificate may be replaced incorrectly.

An application may cache an old value.

Configuration is code with operational consequences.

It should be versioned, reviewed, validated, and rolled out carefully.

---

# Incident Ownership Gaps Slow Recovery

Kubernetes incidents often cross team boundaries.

The application team sees failed requests.

The platform team sees healthy nodes.

The network team sees no packet loss.

The storage team sees acceptable latency.

Each team concludes that its layer is not the problem.

Meanwhile, the customer remains affected.

This is one of the most dangerous incident patterns.

Every team has partial evidence.

No one owns the complete outcome.

Strong organizations define incident ownership by customer impact, not by component boundaries.

Someone must coordinate the whole investigation.

---

# The Platform Team Cannot Own Every Application Failure

Platform teams are often treated as the default escalation path for anything running on Kubernetes.

That model does not scale.

Application teams must understand enough Kubernetes to investigate:

* Pod status
* Logs
* Events
* Probes
* Resources
* Services
* Basic networking
* Deployment history

Platform teams should own the shared platform.

Application teams should own application behavior.

During complex incidents, both must work together.

Clear boundaries improve speed.

Rigid boundaries slow recovery.

---

# Troubleshooting Should Follow Evidence

Random restarts are not a troubleshooting strategy.

Deleting Pods may remove useful evidence.

Restarting CoreDNS may hide the real problem.

Scaling workloads may increase pressure.

Changing several things at once destroys causality.

A disciplined Kubernetes investigation should:

1. Confirm customer impact.
2. Identify the failing path.
3. Check recent changes.
4. Inspect workload status.
5. Review events and logs.
6. Validate Services and endpoints.
7. Test DNS and connectivity.
8. Check probes and resource pressure.
9. Inspect dependencies.
10. Mitigate carefully.
11. Preserve evidence.
12. Confirm recovery end to end.

The goal is not to make the alert disappear.

The goal is to understand what failed and why.

---

# Start With the Customer Path

A strong investigation begins with the actual transaction that is failing.

For example:

Client
↓
DNS
↓
Load Balancer
↓
Ingress
↓
Service
↓
Endpoint
↓
Pod
↓
Application
↓
Database

Test each step.

Do not assume the next layer works because the previous one does.

This method reduces guesswork.

It also prevents teams from focusing too early on the wrong component.

---

# Recent Changes Matter

Many incidents follow change.

A new deployment.

A ConfigMap update.

A policy change.

A certificate rotation.

A node replacement.

A cluster upgrade.

A new storage class.

A modified Ingress rule.

Recent change does not always mean root cause.

But it is one of the strongest investigation signals.

Teams should correlate:

* Deployment history
* Configuration changes
* Infrastructure changes
* Cluster events
* Alert timing
* Customer reports

The timeline often reveals the answer faster than raw metrics alone.

---

# Healthy Infrastructure Does Not Guarantee a Healthy Service

A cluster can report:

* Healthy control plane
* Healthy nodes
* Running Pods
* Available replicas

The customer can still experience an outage.

Platform health and service health are different.

The platform may be functioning correctly while the application is misconfigured.

The application may be healthy while a shared dependency is failing.

The infrastructure may be available while customer workflows are broken.

This is why reliability must be measured from the customer perspective.

---

# Kubernetes Events Tell Part of the Story

Events are extremely useful for identifying:

* Scheduling failures
* Probe failures
* Image-pull errors
* Volume-mount problems
* Evictions
* Node pressure
* Failed scaling

But they are not the entire investigation.

Events show what Kubernetes observed.

They may not show why the application behaved incorrectly.

Teams should combine:

* Events
* Logs
* Metrics
* Traces
* Deployment history
* Dependency data
* Customer symptoms

No single source tells the whole story.

---

# The Best Troubleshooters Think Across Layers

Kubernetes specialists are valuable.

Application specialists are valuable.

Network specialists are valuable.

The strongest incident responders connect these perspectives.

They understand that:

* A probe is application logic expressed through Kubernetes
* A Service is networking controlled by labels
* Storage performance affects application latency
* Autoscaling affects downstream systems
* DNS failures appear as application timeouts
* Cloud infrastructure shapes cluster behavior

They do not stop when their own layer looks healthy.

They follow the evidence until the customer path is restored.

---

# What Mature Kubernetes Incident Response Looks Like

Mature teams prepare before failure.

They maintain:

* Service ownership records
* Dependency maps
* Runbooks
* Standard dashboards
* Known failure modes
* Escalation paths
* Rollback procedures
* Recovery tests
* Incident roles
* Postmortem practices

They also practice.

Game days.

Node-failure exercises.

DNS-failure simulations.

Storage recovery tests.

Cluster upgrade rehearsals.

Readiness-probe validation.

The goal is not to predict every incident.

The goal is to build the capability to respond when the unexpected happens.

---

# Questions to Ask During a Kubernetes Incident

Useful questions include:

* What customer action is failing?
* When did the problem begin?
* What changed recently?
* Are all users affected or only some?
* Is the failure internal, external, or both?
* Does the Service have endpoints?
* Are Pods ready?
* Are probes failing?
* Are Pods restarting or being evicted?
* Is DNS resolving correctly?
* Can the source reach the destination?
* Is a NetworkPolicy blocking traffic?
* Is storage available and performing normally?
* Are resources exhausted or throttled?
* Are dependencies healthy?
* Is the rollout safe to reverse?
* Who owns the complete incident?

These questions move the investigation from assumption to evidence.

---

# Conclusion

Kubernetes failures rarely stay inside Kubernetes because Kubernetes sits in the middle of everything.

Applications.

Networks.

Storage.

Cloud infrastructure.

Security controls.

Deployment systems.

Teams.

A failure in one layer often appears somewhere else.

A label mismatch looks like a network outage.

A readiness failure looks like an application outage.

A storage problem looks like a scheduling problem.

A DNS failure looks like dozens of application failures.

A cloud load-balancer issue looks like Kubernetes is broken.

This is why Kubernetes operations require more than knowledge of Kubernetes objects.

They require systems thinking.

The strongest engineers trace the full customer path.

They validate every layer.

They preserve evidence.

They understand dependencies.

They resist random fixes.

They coordinate teams around customer impact.

Kubernetes can restart workloads.

It cannot replace judgment.
