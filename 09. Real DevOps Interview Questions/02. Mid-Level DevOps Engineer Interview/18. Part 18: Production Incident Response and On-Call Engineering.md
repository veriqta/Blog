# Part 18: Production Incident Response and On-Call Engineering

This section should feel like a real production on-call interview.

The candidate should not answer with:

```text
I would check the logs.
I would restart the service.
I would find the root cause.
```

The interviewer should expect the candidate to explain what they would do in the first minute, first five minutes, first fifteen minutes, and after stabilization.

The core principle is:

```text
During an active incident:

Protect users first.
Stabilize the system second.
Find the full root cause after the immediate danger is controlled.
```

A strong mid-level engineer should be able to move through:

```text
ALERT
  |
  v
VALIDATE
  |
  v
DECLARE
  |
  v
ASSESS BLAST RADIUS
  |
  v
TRIAGE
  |
  v
MITIGATE
  |
  v
VERIFY
  |
  v
INVESTIGATE
  |
  v
RECOVER
  |
  v
POSTMORTEM
  |
  v
CORRECTIVE ACTIONS
```

# 1. Major Production Outage

## Question 1: You are on call. At 02:13, multiple critical alerts fire and customers report that the main application is unavailable. What do you do?

### Interview Answer

I would avoid immediately changing production.

First, I establish whether the alert represents a real incident.

### Minute 0 to 2

Check the highest-level user-facing indicators:

```text
Availability
HTTP error rate
Request latency
Synthetic checks
Traffic
```

I want to answer:

```text
Is production actually down?

Is this monitoring failure?

How many users are affected?
```

If availability suddenly changed:

```text
99.99% -> 35%
```

and synthetic probes are failing, I treat it as a real production incident.

### Minute 2 to 5

I determine scope:

```text
All regions?
One region?
One AZ?
One service?
One endpoint?
One customer group?
```

I also check recent changes:

```text
Deployments
Configuration
Infrastructure
Feature flags
Database changes
Certificates
DNS
```

If the incident is sufficiently severe, I declare it and establish incident coordination.

### Minute 5 to 10

I identify the fastest safe mitigation.

If a deployment occurred immediately before the outage and evidence strongly correlates it with the failure, rollback may be appropriate.

If not, I avoid assuming the latest deployment is automatically responsible.

### Minute 10 onward

Once impact is reduced, I investigate the deeper failure.

The priorities are:

```text
1. Confirm
2. Scope
3. Contain
4. Mitigate
5. Verify
6. Investigate
```

# 2. Incident Severity

## Question 2: How would you determine incident severity?

### Interview Answer

Severity should reflect business and customer impact rather than how technically interesting the failure is.

I consider:

```text
Users affected
Critical functionality affected
Geographic scope
Data integrity risk
Security implications
Revenue impact
Duration
Available workaround
```

For example:

```text
SEV-1

Complete production outage
Critical transaction failure
Major security incident
Large-scale customer impact
```

versus:

```text
SEV-2

Significant degradation
Partial functionality unavailable
Regional failure
Meaningful but limited impact
```

Exact definitions should follow the organization's incident policy.

---

## Question 3: Why does severity classification matter?

### Interview Answer

Because severity can determine:

```text
Who gets paged
Escalation speed
Incident leadership
Communication frequency
Executive involvement
Customer communication
Postmortem requirements
```

It creates a shared understanding of urgency.

---

# 3. Incident Commander

## Question 4: What is the role of an incident commander?

### Interview Answer

The incident commander coordinates the response.

They should not necessarily be the person typing commands into production.

Responsibilities may include:

```text
Set priorities
Assign investigation areas
Track mitigation
Control communication
Request specialists
Maintain situational awareness
Prevent duplicated work
Drive decisions
```

A useful model is:

```text
              INCIDENT COMMANDER
                     |
        +------------+------------+
        |            |            |
        v            v            v
   Operations    Application   Communications
   Investigation Investigation
```

Without coordination, ten engineers may investigate the same thing while nobody checks the actual failing dependency.

---

## Question 5: Why shouldn't everyone make production changes during a major incident?

### Interview Answer

Because uncontrolled simultaneous changes destroy the ability to understand cause and effect.

For example:

```text
Engineer A restarts Pods
Engineer B changes timeout
Engineer C scales database
Engineer D changes routing
Engineer E rolls back
```

If production recovers, nobody knows which action worked.

Worse, the changes can interact and increase impact.

Major changes should be coordinated and recorded.

---

# 4. Triage

## Question 6: What does incident triage mean to you?

### Interview Answer

Triage means rapidly narrowing the problem enough to choose the next useful action.

I ask:

```text
What changed?

What is failing?

What remains healthy?

When did it start?

Where is it happening?

Who is affected?

What dependency is common?
```

I do not attempt to understand every technical detail immediately.

The goal is decision-quality information.

# 5. Blast Radius

## Question 7: How do you determine blast radius?

### Interview Answer

I segment the impact.

For example:

```text
Region
AZ
Cluster
Service
Version
Endpoint
Customer
Tenant
Request type
Device
Dependency
```

Suppose global error rate is:

```text
8%
```

That could mean:

```text
Every user:
8% failure
```

or:

```text
One region:
80% failure

Other regions:
0%
```

Those require very different responses.

# 6. Mitigation Versus Root Cause

## Question 8: What is the difference between mitigation and root-cause resolution?

### Interview Answer

Mitigation reduces customer impact.

Root-cause resolution removes the underlying problem.

Suppose:

```text
Memory leak
    |
    v
Application OOM
    |
    v
Customer errors
```

Restarting the affected workloads may temporarily restore service.

That is mitigation.

Fixing the code causing the memory leak is root-cause resolution.

During a severe incident, waiting for the permanent fix before mitigating can unnecessarily extend customer impact.

---

## Question 9: Would you restart a service if you did not understand why it failed?

### Interview Answer

Potentially, if:

```text
Impact is severe
Restart is understood and low risk
It is likely to restore service
Necessary evidence has been captured
```

But I would recognize that restart may destroy useful evidence.

Where possible, I capture:

```text
Logs
Memory/resource state
Process information
Metrics
Events
```

before restarting.

# 7. Rollback Decisions

## Question 10: When would you rollback during an incident?

### Interview Answer

A rollback becomes attractive when:

```text
Failure started after deployment

New version shows worse health

Previous version was known healthy

Rollback is understood

Database/schema compatibility permits it
```

I compare:

```text
Version A
Healthy

Version B
Unhealthy
```

before making the decision.

---

## Question 11: Why might rollback be unsafe?

### Interview Answer

Because deployment may have changed:

```text
Database schema
Persistent data
Message formats
External state
Configuration
Infrastructure
```

The previous application version may no longer be compatible.

Rollback should be designed before incidents occur.

# 8. Failed Rollback

## Question 12: You rollback, but production becomes worse. What do you do?

### Interview Answer

I stop assuming rollback is the solution.

### Minute 0 to 2 after failed rollback

Determine what changed:

```text
Did old version start?

Are health checks passing?

Is database compatible?

Are configuration versions compatible?

Are Pods actually serving traffic?
```

### Minute 2 to 5

If rollback introduced additional failure, stop further uncontrolled rollout.

Consider:

```text
Pause deployment
Restore known compatible configuration
Shift traffic
Disable affected feature
Fail over
```

### Minute 5 onward

Reassess the incident from the current production state.

A failed rollback is now part of the incident.

# 9. Dependency Outage

## Question 13: Your application is healthy, but an external payment provider is failing. What do you do?

### Interview Answer

First determine:

```text
Failure rate
Latency
Affected transactions
Regions
Provider status
```

Then protect the application from dependency failure.

Potential controls include:

```text
Timeouts
Circuit breakers
Retry limits
Backoff
Fallback
Queueing
Graceful degradation
Alternative provider
```

The dangerous response is unlimited retries.

# 10. Cascading Failure

## Question 14: Explain how a dependency failure can become a cascading failure.

### Interview Answer

Example:

```text
Database slows
    |
    v
API requests wait
    |
    v
Connection pools fill
    |
    v
Requests timeout
    |
    v
Clients retry
    |
    v
Traffic increases
    |
    v
API saturates
    |
    v
More timeouts
```

The original database problem becomes a platform-wide incident.

The response must address both:

```text
Original failure
+
Amplification mechanisms
```

# 11. Traffic Spike Scenario

## Question 15: Traffic suddenly increases 8x and production starts failing. Walk me through the first 20 minutes.

### Interview Answer

### Minute 0 to 2

Confirm:

```text
Traffic actually increased?
Legitimate or suspicious?
Which endpoints?
Which regions?
```

Check:

```text
Request rate
Error rate
Latency
Saturation
```

### Minute 2 to 5

Identify the first saturated component:

```text
Load balancer
Application
Database
Cache
Queue
External dependency
```

### Minute 5 to 10

Protect the system.

Depending on architecture:

```text
Scale workloads
Rate limit
Reject non-critical traffic
Enable caching
Disable expensive features
Protect dependencies
```

### Minute 10 to 20

Verify whether:

```text
Error rate decreases
Latency recovers
Queues stabilize
Database recovers
```

Scaling application Pods is useless if the database is already saturated.

# 12. Database Saturation

## Question 16: Database CPU reaches 100%, latency rises, and application errors begin. What do you do minute by minute?

### Interview Answer

### Minute 0 to 3

Determine whether saturation is caused by:

```text
Traffic increase
Expensive query
Locking
Connection storm
Deployment
Background job
```

Check:

```text
Connections
Query latency
Slow queries
Locks
CPU
I/O
```

### Minute 3 to 7

Reduce pressure.

Potential actions:

```text
Rate limit
Pause background jobs
Disable expensive feature
Reduce retries
Use cache
Shift read traffic
```

### Minute 7 to 15

Identify dominant queries or workloads.

If one new query consumes most database resources, stopping that source may restore service faster than increasing database size.

### After stabilization

Investigate:

```text
Query design
Indexes
Connection management
Capacity
Caching
Traffic behavior
```

# 13. Memory Leak

## Question 17: Application memory steadily increases until Pods are OOMKilled every few hours. What do you do?

### Interview Answer

First establish:

```text
Is memory proportional to traffic?

Does it return after traffic falls?

Which version introduced it?

Heap or non-heap?

All Pods or specific Pods?
```

During active impact, mitigation might include:

```text
Rollback
Controlled restart
Temporary scaling
Traffic reduction
```

But increasing memory limits indefinitely is not a root-cause fix.

I preserve evidence for application debugging before restarting where practical.

# 14. Queue Backlog

## Question 18: Queue depth jumps from 5,000 to 2 million messages. What do you do?

### Interview Answer

I compare:

```text
Producer rate
Consumer rate
Processing latency
Consumer errors
Oldest message age
```

The key equation is:

```text
Incoming rate > Processing rate
           |
           v
       Backlog grows
```

### First five minutes

Determine whether:

```text
Producers increased
Consumers decreased
Dependency slowed
Messages are failing/retrying
```

### Next ten minutes

Depending on cause:

```text
Scale consumers
Fix failed consumers
Reduce production rate
Pause non-critical producers
Protect downstream dependency
```

But scaling consumers aggressively can destroy a database or external API if that dependency is already saturated.

# 15. Partial Regional Failure

## Question 19: One region is failing while two other regions remain healthy. What do you do?

### Interview Answer

First quantify:

```text
Region A:
60% errors

Region B:
0.2%

Region C:
0.1%
```

Then compare regional dependencies:

```text
Network
Database
DNS
Cloud services
Configuration
Deployment
Capacity
```

If architecture supports safe failover, shifting traffic away from the unhealthy region may be the fastest mitigation.

But I verify whether healthy regions can absorb the additional load first.

---

## Question 20: Why can regional failover create another outage?

### Interview Answer

Because:

```text
Region A fails
     |
     v
Traffic moves to B + C
     |
     v
B + C exceed capacity
     |
     v
Global outage
```

Failover capacity must be planned and tested.

# 16. Communication During Incidents

## Question 21: What should incident communication contain?

### Interview Answer

Communication should be factual and concise.

For example:

```text
14:10

Checkout failures affecting
approximately 35% of EU traffic.

Payment dependency latency elevated.

Traffic mitigation in progress.

Next update: 14:20.
```

Avoid speculation presented as fact.

---

## Question 22: Why should technical responders avoid constant status messaging?

### Interview Answer

Because communication can consume the engineers who need to investigate.

The incident structure should allow someone to handle communication while responders focus on mitigation.

# 18. Postmortems

## Question 24: What makes a useful incident postmortem?

### Interview Answer

It should explain:

```text
What happened
Customer impact
Timeline
Technical cause
Contributing factors
Detection
Response
What worked
What failed
Corrective actions
```

The goal is learning and system improvement.

---

## Question 25: Why shouldn't the postmortem simply say "engineer made a mistake"?

### Interview Answer

Because that does not explain why the system allowed one mistake to become a production outage.

Ask:

```text
Why was the change possible?

Why did validation miss it?

Why did rollout expose everyone?

Why didn't monitoring detect it earlier?

Why was recovery difficult?
```

# 20. Preventing Recurrence

## Question 27: How do you prevent recurrence without trying to eliminate every possible failure?

### Interview Answer

I improve layers of protection.

```text
PREVENT
   |
   v
DETECT
   |
   v
LIMIT BLAST RADIUS
   |
   v
MITIGATE
   |
   v
RECOVER
```

Not every failure can be prevented.

Production engineering also requires making failures smaller, easier to detect, and faster to recover from.

# Postmortem

The weak conclusion would be:

```text
A reporting query overloaded
the database.
```

The stronger analysis asks:

```text
Why could reporting use
the production transactional database?

Why was there no query limit?

Why did the scheduled job run
during this period?

Why didn't database saturation
alert before customer impact?

Why did queue recovery
extend the incident?

Why wasn't reporting isolated?
```

# The Mid-Level On-Call Decision Model

During an incident, think:

```text
1. ARE USERS AFFECTED?
        |
        v
2. HOW BAD IS IT?
        |
        v
3. WHAT IS THE BLAST RADIUS?
        |
```

# What a Mid-Level Candidate Should Demonstrate

A junior candidate may say:

```text
"The application is down.
I'll restart it."
```

A mid-level candidate asks:

```text
What is the blast radius?

What evidence shows
the application itself
is the failing component?
```

A junior candidate may say:

```text
"I need to find the root cause
before doing anything."
```

A mid-level candidate understands:

```text
During severe customer impact:

Mitigation can come
before complete root-cause analysis.
```

A junior candidate may say:

```text
"The deployment happened
five minutes ago.
Rollback immediately."
```

A mid-level candidate asks:

```text
Is the new version actually failing?

Is rollback safe?

Were there state or schema changes?
```

A junior candidate may say:

```text

Incident resolved."
```

A mid-level candidate verifies:

```text

```


A mid-level candidate asks:

```text

handle 10x consumption?
```

```text
"Fail over the broken region."
```

A mid-level candidate asks:

```text
Can the healthy regions
absorb the traffic?
```

# Final Takeaway

Production incident response is not a race to type commands.

A strong on-call engineer repeatedly makes decisions from evidence:

```text
DETECT
   |
   v
CONFIRM
   |
   v
DECLARE
   |
   v
SCOPE
   |
   v
COORDINATE
   |
   v
MITIGATE
   |
   v
VERIFY
   |
   v
INVESTIGATE
   |
   v
RECOVER
   |
   v
LEARN
```

A strong mid-level DevOps engineer should be able to explain, minute by minute:

```text
What do I check first?

What evidence do I need?

Who is affected?

How large is the blast radius?

Do I rollback?

What if rollback fails?

Can I fail over safely?

What dependency is failing?

Is retry behavior amplifying it?

What should I stop?

What should I scale?

What should I protect?

How do I communicate?

How do I know mitigation worked?

When are users actually recovered?

What is the root cause?

What allowed the failure to spread?

What corrective actions prevent recurrence?
```

During an active incident, the first objective is not to produce the perfect explanation.

It is to reduce customer impact safely, preserve control of the system, and create enough evidence to understand exactly what happened afterward.
